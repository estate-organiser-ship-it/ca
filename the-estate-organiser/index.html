The plan

A will says who gets what. It does not say what to do next.

The Organiser carries the whole path your executor walks, in the order they meet it. Every step below is already written out in the book, and the first stage is in the free Executor Toolkit.

STAGE 1

The first 48 hours

Secure · Notify · Record

  • Secure the home, the mail, the pets
  • Find the will, and find this book
  • Obtain the death certificate and register the death
  • Ask the funeral director about extra certified copies
  • Tell the employer: final pay, leave, retirement savings

Without a planThe will found too late, one certified copy ordered when six were needed, valuables unrecorded.

STAGE 2

The first month

Notify · Claim · Protect

  • Apply for probate where it is required
  • Notify the banks, the registered plans, Service Canada, the insurers
  • Cancel or transfer the services
  • Claim the life insurance and the survivor benefits
  • Identify the debts, keep the urgent bills paid

Without a planThe death re-explained on call after call, an old registered plan never claimed, an account frozen while the bills keep arriving.

STAGE 3

The first year

Settle · Account · Distribute

  • The grant, then an estate bank account
  • Collect the assets, pay the debts in the right order
  • File the final tax return, deal with the property
  • Prepare the estate accounts, obtain CRA clearance
  • Distribute, then keep the records

Whether probate is required, where you apply and how long it takes all vary from province to province, and every estate is different. The Organiser points your executor to the right professional at each step rather than guessing on their behalf.

The gap

What you leave sits in three systems that do not talk to each other.

No bank, no plan provider and no government department is in charge of joining them up. That job lands on the people you love, in the middle of their grief.

The will

It names who inherits. It does not tell anyone where the accounts are, what the passwords are, or what you wanted. Your executor can hold a valid will and still have no idea where to start.

In the Organiser: where the will is kept, who the executor is, and the message written to them.

Registered accounts and pensions

In common-law provinces an RRSP, RRIF or TFSA is generally paid to the beneficiary named on the account, outside the will. Quebec works differently. Either way, that form is the classic set-and-forget, and most families have never checked it.

In the Organiser: every plan, every policy and the beneficiary named on each, in one list.

Accounts and the home

Accounts, insurance, the mortgage, the utilities, the digital life. Each one is held by a different organisation, each with its own process, and none of them will call your family first.

In the Organiser: every account, every login, how the home is held, and who to contact for each.

Questions

Good to know

I have already got a will. Do I still need this?

A will says who inherits. It doesn't tell anyone where your accounts are, what your passwords are, which registered plans exist, or what you wanted for your funeral. Your executor can be holding a perfectly valid will and still have no idea where to start. The Organiser is the document that sits beside the will and answers all of that. The two do different jobs.

Is most of this free on the government websites?

Some of it is, and where a government service is the right answer the book points you at it. What no government site does is put your accounts, your passwords, your plans, your wishes and your contacts into one document your family can pick up and follow. The information is scattered across a dozen departments by design. The Organiser is the one place it all comes together, in your handwriting.

We are not wealthy. Is this really for us?

It matters more, not less. A large estate usually comes with a lawyer and an accountant already involved. A modest one is the one where a forgotten account, a joint account or a missing password does real damage, and where there is no professional standing by to sort it out. The Organiser is for the family that has to do it themselves.

Is it a bit morbid?

Most people say the opposite once they start. It stops being about dying somewhere around the second page and becomes about tidying up: the accounts you forgot, the subscription still billing, the plan from a job two decades ago. Readers describe finishing it as a relief. It is a practical afternoon, not a sad one.

Is this another subscription that will bill me every year?

No. One payment, once. No account to log into, no renewal, no annual fee. Updates to the document are yours to keep.

How long does it take to fill in?

Most people work through it over a few weeks, not a weekend, one section at a time, when you've got the energy. There's no rush, and you can save and come back any time.

It looks big. Do I really have to fill in all of it?

No. It is big because it covers a whole life, and you have had a lot of life. But not every section applies to everyone. Every section carries a “not for me” tick, and the contents page keeps track. Some sections take a while, some take a minute, some you never open. What matters is that every section you could need is in there.

Is my information safe?

Yes. It's a file you fill in on your own computer, or print and fill in by hand. Nothing is stored online or in anyone's cloud, so there's nothing to be breached. Where you keep it is entirely up to you.

Is this legal or financial advice?

No, and that's by design. The Estate Organiser is a place to record your information and wishes. It isn't a Will, and it doesn't replace your lawyer or accountant. It simply makes sure that when your family needs those professionals, they can find everything fast. Rules vary across Canadian provinces and territories, so confirm the specifics with your own advisors.

Does it work in my province?

Yes. It's about gathering your accounts, passwords, documents and wishes in one place, which matters everywhere. Where province-specific steps come up, it points you to the right professional rather than guessing for you.
