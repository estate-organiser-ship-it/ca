What this actually is

A will says who gets what. It does not say where anything is.

What you leave sits across three systems that do not talk to each other, and nobody is in charge of joining them up. That job lands on the people you love, in the worst week of their lives.

The will

It names who inherits. It does not say where the accounts are, what the passwords are, or what you wanted. An executor can hold a perfectly valid will and still have no idea where to start.

In the Organiser: where the will is kept, who the executor is, and the message written to them.

Registered accounts and pensions

In common-law provinces an RRSP, RRIF or TFSA is generally paid to the beneficiary named on the account, outside the will. Quebec works differently. Either way, the form is the classic set-and-forget, and most families have never checked it.

In the Organiser: every plan, every policy and the beneficiary named on each, in one list.

Accounts and the home

Banking, insurance, the mortgage, the utilities, the digital life. Every one held by a different organisation, every one with its own process, and not one of them will call your family first.

In the Organiser: every account, every login, how the home is held, and who to contact for each.

The plan inside it

It carries the whole path, in the order your family meets it.

Not a list of chapters. The actual sequence, from the first few hours through to closing the estate. The first stage of it is in the free toolkit, so you can read it before you buy anything.

STAGE 1

The first 48 hours

Secure · Notify · Record

Secure the home, the mail, the pets. Find the will, and find this book. The death certificate, then registering the death with the funeral director. Tell the employer about final pay, leave and retirement savings. Record the valuables.

STAGE 2

The first month

Notify · Claim · Protect

Apply for probate where it is needed. The banks, the registered plans, Service Canada, the insurers. Cancel or transfer the services. Claim the life insurance and the survivor benefits. Identify the debts and keep the urgent bills paid.

STAGE 3

The first year

Settle · Account · Distribute

The grant, then an estate bank account. Collect the assets, pay the debts in the right order, file the final tax return, deal with the property. Prepare the estate accounts, obtain CRA clearance, then distribute and keep the records.

Whether probate is required, where you apply and how long it takes all vary from province to province, and every estate is different. The Organiser points your executor to the right professional at each step rather than guessing on their behalf.

Questions

Good to know.

The things people ask before they decide.

I have already got a will. Do I still need this?

A will says who inherits. It does not tell anyone where your accounts are, what your passwords are, which registered plans exist, or what you wanted for your funeral. Your executor can be holding a perfectly valid will and still have no idea where to start. The Organiser sits beside the will and answers all of that.

Is most of this free on the government websites?

Some of it is, and where a government service is the right answer we point you at it. What no government site does is put your accounts, your passwords, your plans, your wishes and your contacts into one document your family can pick up and follow. The information is scattered across a dozen departments by design.

We are not wealthy. Is this really for us?

It matters more, not less. A large estate usually has a lawyer and an accountant already involved. A modest one is where a forgotten account or a missing password does real damage, and where there is no professional standing by to sort it out.

Every province is different. How does one book cover that?

What the book records is the same everywhere: your accounts, your plans, your passwords, your wishes, your people. That does not change at a provincial border. Where the rules do differ, probate, registering a death, some of the forms, the Organiser points your executor to the right office in your province rather than guessing on their behalf.

Is it a bit morbid?

It feels that way for about ten minutes. Then it feels like relief. The morbid version is the one where your family is guessing, ringing around, and opening drawers. Most readers tell us the odd thing is how calm they felt once it was done.

How long does it take to fill in?

Most people work through it over a few weeks, not a weekend, one section at a time, when you've got the energy. There's no rush, and you can save and come back any time.

It looks big. Do I really have to fill in all of it?

No. It is big because it covers a whole life, and you have had a lot of life. But not every section applies to everyone. Every section carries a “not for me” tick, and the contents page keeps track. Some sections take a while, some take a minute, some you never open. What matters is that every section you could need is in there.

Is my information safe?

Yes. It's a file you fill in on your own computer, or print and fill in by hand. Nothing is stored online or in anyone's cloud, so there's nothing to be breached. Where you keep it is entirely up to you.

Is this a subscription?

No. One payment, once. No account to log into, no renewal, no annual fee. Updates to the document are yours to keep.

Is this legal or financial advice?

No, and that's by design. The Estate Organiser is a place to record your information and wishes. It isn't a Will, and it doesn't replace your lawyer or accountant. It simply makes sure that when your family needs those professionals, they can find everything fast. Rules vary across Canadian provinces and territories, so confirm the specifics with your own advisors.
